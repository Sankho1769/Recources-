01 · About

About eggs

An egg is a protective package laid by a bird, holding everything a developing chick would need. Most eggs sold for eating come from hens and are unfertilised.

How eggs are produced. Hens kept for egg production lay regularly, and each egg takes roughly a day to form. Eggs are then collected, checked, graded by size and quality, packed and, in many countries, cooled and washed before they reach shops. Rules for grading, washing and labelling differ between countries.

What is inside. A hard shell encloses two membranes, the white (albumen) and the yolk. A small air pocket at the wide end grows as an egg ages, which is why older eggs behave differently when tested in water.

Why cooks use them. Eggs are easy to cook on their own and also do useful jobs in other food: they bind ingredients, trap air when whisked, thicken sauces and custards as they set, and give baked goods colour.

Everyday uses

  • Boiled, fried, poached or scrambled
  • Folded into omelettes and frittatas
  • Binding, thickening and baking
  • Curries, sandwiches and salads

02 · Types

Types of eggs

Different eggs suit different dishes. This is a description of differences, not a ranking.

01 · Most widely sold

Chicken eggs

The everyday egg in most kitchens and shops, sold in several sizes.

Shells are white or brown depending on the breed of hen, and colour does not by itself indicate flavour or quality. A large chicken egg weighs about 50 g in USDA nutrition data; size grades differ between countries.

02 · Larger

Duck eggs

Larger than chicken eggs, with a pale, often bluish-green shell.

A duck egg weighs about 70 g in USDA data. They can be used like chicken eggs in many dishes, and flavour and texture differ. Cook them thoroughly, as you would any egg.

03 · Smaller

Quail eggs

Very small eggs with speckled shells, often used whole.

A quail egg weighs about 9 g in USDA data, so several make a portion. They suit garnishes, salads and snacks, and cook faster than chicken eggs because of their size.

Also on the label

  • Free-range eggs

    A label describing how hens are housed, for example with access to outdoor space. The exact rules depend on the country and the scheme, so check the pack.

  • Organic eggs

    A label describing how the hens are kept and fed under an organic standard. It describes farming method. This guide makes no claim that these eggs are nutritionally different.

03 · Nutrition

What is inside an egg

The white is mostly water and protein. Most of the fat and fat-soluble vitamins are in the yolk.

Protein
Both the white and the yolk contain protein, which the body uses to build and repair tissue.
Fats
Mostly in the yolk, a mix of saturated and unsaturated fats. Eggs also naturally contain dietary cholesterol.
Vitamins
USDA data for whole eggs lists vitamin A, vitamin D, vitamin B12, riboflavin (B2) and folate, among others, along with choline.
Minerals
Includes iron, zinc, phosphorus, selenium, calcium and potassium.

04 · Benefits

Eggs in a balanced diet

General information, not medical advice.

  • A source of protein

    A large egg provides about 6 g of protein, so eggs can add protein to breakfast, lunch or dinner.

  • A range of nutrients

    Eggs contain a variety of vitamins and minerals alongside protein and fat, as shown in the nutrition section.

  • Versatile

    They can be boiled, fried, scrambled, baked or used to bind and thicken, so they fit many cuisines and budgets.

  • Quick to prepare

    Many egg dishes take only minutes, and eggs pair well with vegetables, grains, bread and legumes.

  • One food among many

    No single food makes a diet healthy. How many eggs suit you depends on your overall diet, health and local dietary guidance. If you have a medical condition or an egg allergy, follow your doctor’s or dietitian’s advice.

05 · Storage

Freshness, storage and hygiene

Based on USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service guidance.

Choosing
Buy eggs from a place that keeps them cool. Open the carton and check that the shells are clean and uncracked. Do not buy or use cracked or leaking eggs.
Reading the carton
Look for the date and any storage instructions. Date labels differ between countries, so follow the one on your pack. Raw eggs in the shell are generally best used within 3 to 5 weeks when kept refrigerated.
Storing
Refrigerate at 4 °C (40 °F) or below, in the carton on an inner shelf rather than the door. Do not wash eggs before storing them.
Handling
Wash your hands, utensils and surfaces after touching raw eggs, and keep raw eggs away from ready-to-eat food. Do not leave eggs or egg dishes out for more than two hours.
Cooking safely
Cook eggs until the white is set and the yolk is firm. Egg dishes are safest cooked to 71 °C (160 °F). Young children, older adults, pregnant people and anyone with a weakened immune system should avoid runny eggs.
Leftovers
Refrigerate hard-boiled eggs within two hours and eat them within a week. Do not freeze eggs in the shell.

The water test. Place an egg in a bowl of cold water. A fresh egg sinks and lies flat; an older egg tilts or floats because the air pocket has grown. This shows age, not safety. If an egg smells off or looks unusual when cracked, discard it.

06 · Recipes

Simple egg recipes

Five everyday ideas. Open any card for ingredients and method.

01 · Classic

Boiled eggs

Hard- or soft-boiled, ready for breakfast, salads or snacks.

  • Serves 2
  • About 12 minutes
Show recipe: Boiled eggs

Ingredients

  • 4 eggs
  • Water
  • Cold water and ice, for cooling
  • Salt and pepper, to serve

Method

  1. Place the eggs in a saucepan and cover with cold water by about 2.5 cm (1 in).
  2. Bring to a gentle boil, then reduce the heat to a steady simmer.
  3. Cook for about 7 minutes for a soft-set yolk or 10–12 minutes for a firm yolk. Anyone at higher risk of foodborne illness should choose firm eggs.
  4. Move the eggs to cold water for a few minutes, then peel and season.
  5. Refrigerate any leftovers in their shells and eat within a week.

02 · Quick

Omelette

A soft folded omelette with whatever herbs or vegetables you have.

  • Serves 1
  • About 8 minutes
Show recipe: Omelette

Ingredients

  • 2–3 eggs
  • 1 tsp butter or oil
  • Salt and pepper
  • Chopped herbs, tomato or cheese (optional)

Method

  1. Beat the eggs with a pinch of salt and pepper until evenly mixed.
  2. Heat the butter or oil in a non-stick pan over medium heat.
  3. Pour in the eggs. As the edges set, gently pull them towards the centre so liquid egg flows underneath.
  4. When the egg is set with no runny liquid left, add your filling to one half, fold over and serve.

03 · Everyday

Scrambled eggs

Soft, creamy curds in a few minutes, good with toast.

  • Serves 2
  • About 6 minutes
Show recipe: Scrambled eggs

Ingredients

  • 4 eggs
  • 1–2 tbsp milk (optional)
  • 1 tsp butter
  • Salt and pepper
  • Toast, to serve

Method

  1. Whisk the eggs with the milk, salt and pepper.
  2. Melt the butter in a pan over low to medium heat and add the eggs.
  3. Stir gently as they thicken, pushing them across the pan in soft folds.
  4. Keep cooking until the eggs are fully set with no visible liquid, then serve straight away.

04 · Comfort

Egg curry

Hard-boiled eggs simmered in a spiced onion and tomato gravy.

  • Serves 3–4
  • About 35 minutes
Show recipe: Egg curry

Ingredients

  • 6 hard-boiled eggs, peeled
  • 2 tbsp oil
  • 2 onions, finely chopped
  • 2 tomatoes, chopped
  • 1 tsp ginger-garlic paste
  • ½ tsp turmeric
  • 1 tsp chilli powder, or to taste
  • 1 tsp ground coriander
  • ½ tsp garam masala
  • Salt, to taste
  • Chopped coriander leaves, to finish

Method

  1. Heat the oil and cook the onions until soft and golden.
  2. Add the ginger-garlic paste and cook for a minute, then stir in the tomatoes and cook until soft.
  3. Add the turmeric, chilli, coriander and salt. Cook until the oil begins to separate, then add about 1 cup of water.
  4. Simmer for 8–10 minutes to a gravy you like. Score the eggs lightly, add them and warm through for a few minutes.
  5. Finish with garam masala and coriander leaves. Serve with rice or flatbread.

05 · Lunchbox

Egg sandwich

Chopped boiled eggs with a creamy dressing between slices of bread.

  • Serves 2
  • About 15 minutes
Show recipe: Egg sandwich

Ingredients

  • 3 hard-boiled eggs, peeled
  • 2 tbsp mayonnaise or thick yogurt
  • Salt and pepper
  • 4 slices of bread
  • Lettuce leaves

Method

  1. Chop or mash the eggs and mix with the mayonnaise or yogurt, salt and pepper.
  2. Layer lettuce and the egg mixture on two slices of bread and top with the others.
  3. Cut in half and eat soon, or keep chilled until you eat it.

07 · FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Do eggs need to be refrigerated?

USDA food-safety guidance is to refrigerate eggs at 40 °F (4 °C) or below. Practices differ between countries, and the carton usually says how to store them, so follow the packaging. In warm weather, refrigerating is the safer choice.

How long do eggs keep?

The USDA advises using raw eggs in the shell within 3 to 5 weeks, and hard-cooked eggs within about a week. Check the date and storage instructions on your carton too.

How can I tell whether an egg is fresh?

Check the date on the carton first. At home, a fresh egg sinks and lies flat in a bowl of water, while an older egg tilts or floats as the air pocket inside grows. This test shows age, not safety. Crack each egg into a separate bowl; if it smells off or looks unusual, throw it away.

What is the difference between brown and white eggs?

Shell colour depends mainly on the breed of hen. It does not by itself tell you about flavour or quality.

Are duck or quail eggs better than chicken eggs?

They are different, not better or worse. Duck eggs are larger and quail eggs are much smaller, and flavour and texture vary. Nutrient values differ by type, breed, feed and the database used, so compare labels or a source such as USDA FoodData Central instead of assuming one is superior. Handle and cook them with the same care as chicken eggs.

Why does my hard-boiled egg have a green ring around the yolk?

A greenish-grey ring usually appears when eggs are cooked for too long or cooled slowly. It is a cosmetic result of overcooking. Shorter cooking and cooling the eggs in cold water straight away help prevent it.

How many eggs can I eat?

There is no single answer. It depends on your overall diet, health and local dietary guidance. If you have a medical condition, for example advice about cholesterol, or an egg allergy, follow the advice of your doctor or dietitian.

Can I freeze eggs?

Do not freeze eggs in their shells. The USDA says raw eggs can be frozen after they are cracked and beaten (whites can also be frozen on their own). Hard-boiled eggs do not freeze well.

Where can I find the full nutrition details?

See the Nutrition section above for a summary, and USDA FoodData Central for complete, searchable values for many egg types and preparations.
